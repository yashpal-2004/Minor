UNIT 1 — Strategic Foundations & Environmental Audit · Sessions 1–12

1 Strategy Basics

  • Strategy = deliberate direction — plans/policies deciding how resources reach goals.
  • Hard work alone ≠ strategy. Strategy = deliberate trade-offs + direction + planned resource use.
  • Strategic management = Planning + Formulation + Decision-Making + Evaluation → long-term sustainability.
  • Chain: Goal → Objective → Strategy → Tactics (what you want → how you get it).
  • Applies to any size business (tailor, tuition teacher, juice stall).
  • Ex: Haldiram's — one 1937 Bikaner shop → ₹12,800 Cr empire via deliberate city-by-city factory expansion.

2 Policy · Program · Procedure

PolicyProgramProcedure
WhatStanding guideline/boundaryTime-bound activity setFixed step-by-step method
DurationIndefiniteStart + endRepeats identically
Ex"Only fresh ingredients""Friday Combo Week"Wash→steam→plate→serve
  • Stanford Prison Experiment (1971, Zimbardo) — no clear policy/procedure → roles spiraled out of control.
  • Businesses that skip writing these down fail quietly — right when they try to grow (new hire/2nd branch).

    • 3–4 The Nine Building Blocks

    Mission — why we exist
    Goal — long-term aim
    Objectives — specific, now
    Policy / Program / Procedure
    Budget — numbers
    Tactic — quick move
    = Strategy
    • Mission: broad, stable, why business exists. Amul (1946) — "fair returns for farmers," unchanged since.
    • Goal vs Objective: Goal = long-term aim (semi-broad); Objective = specific & measurable, changes often.
    • Budget: plan in numbers — forces trade-offs.
    • Tactic: quick, short-term, in-the-moment move (smaller/faster than a program).
    • Common mistake: writing a mission but never linking it to objectives/budget, or running tactics that contradict mission.

    6–9 Three Levels of Strategy + Strategic Mgmt Model

    10 Environmental Scanning & Forecasting

  • Scanning = ongoing habit of watching for change signals before they force your hand. Outward (customers/competitors/tech) + Inward (own capability gaps).
  • Forecasting = turning scanned signals into a usable, honest guess. 3 methods: Trend extrapolation, Expert judgment, Scenario thinking (best/worst/likely).
  • Nokia (2009→): had ~60% India share, missed Android/touchscreen shift by clinging to Symbian → collapsed. Signal was visible for years — lesson: scanning ≠ predicting unknowns, it's noticing the visible.
  • Break-even = Fixed Costs ÷ Profit per unit. (e.g. ₹30,000 ÷ ₹48/plate ≈ 625 plates/month ≈ 21/day)

    • 11 Porter's Five Forces

    New EntrantsHow easily can a new player join?
    Buyer PowerCan buyers switch/demand low prices?
    Supplier PowerCan suppliers squeeze margins?
    RivalryHow fierce is price/ad/innovation fight?
    SubstitutesDiff. product, same need — most overlooked!

    Patanjali case: disrupted decades-stable Indian FMCG (HUL) via Ayurvedic/swadeshi positioning → proved even stable industries face new entrants. Ratings: Entrants HIGH Buyers HIGH Rivalry HIGH

    Momo cart: Entrants HIGH, Substitutes HIGH, Suppliers LOW

  • Weak forces → even average execution wins. Strong forces → punish even brilliant execution.

    • 12 SWOT & Industry Analysis

    Strengths (internal) — genuinely good at; must be rare/hard-to-copy
    Weaknesses (internal) — genuinely vulnerable, name honestly
    Opportunities (external) — changes in your favour
    Threats (external) — changes against you

    Same external event can be O for one biz, T for another.

    SWOTFive Forces
    ScopeOne businessWhole category structure
    NatureInternal + external mixPurely external/structural
    UseQuick, personal starting pointRigorous, cross-category comparison
  • Industry analysis: zooms out to whole industry size/growth/players (vs SWOT = one biz).
  • Mistakes: generic strengths, confusing weakness↔threat, too-long lists, never updating.

    • 13 Organizational Resources, Capabilities & Core Competencies

    • Resources = inputs a firm owns/controls — Tangible (cash, plant, land, machinery) vs Intangible (brand, patents, culture, know-how, reputation).
    • Capability = a firm's capacity to deploy resources together, purposefully, to achieve a desired outcome — resources alone do nothing without the capability to combine them.
    • Core Competence = a bundle of skills/capabilities that (a) is hard to imitate, (b) creates real customer value, and (c) can be leveraged across multiple products/markets.
    • Ex: Honda's core competence = small-engine design → used across bikes, cars, generators, lawnmowers, boats.
    • Not every strength is a core competence — it must pass all three tests above, or it's just "something we're okay at."

    13 VRIO Framework

    Tests whether a resource/capability can actually deliver sustained competitive advantage — asked in sequence:

    TestQuestionIf "No"
    ValuableDoes it help exploit an opportunity / neutralize a threat?Competitive disadvantage
    RareDo few/no competitors have it?Competitive parity only
    InimitableIs it costly/hard for rivals to copy or substitute?Temporary advantage only
    OrganizedIs the firm structured/managed to actually exploit it?Unused advantage (wasted potential)
  • Ex: Coca-Cola's formula is Valuable + Rare + Inimitable — but without global bottling/distribution (Organized), it would be wasted potential.
  • Passing all 4 = Sustained Competitive Advantage. Missing just "Organized" is the most common real-world failure point.

    • 14 Value Chain Analysis

    Porter's model — breaks the firm into discrete activities to see where value/margin is actually created.

    Primary ActivitiesInbound Logistics → Operations → Outbound Logistics → Marketing & Sales → Service
    Support ActivitiesFirm Infrastructure, HR Management, Technology Development, Procurement
  • Goal: find which activities create the most value vs cost the most — then protect/strengthen the value-creating ones and cut/outsource the rest.
  • Ex: Zara's value chain edge is in Outbound Logistics + Operations (fast replenishment, near-shore manufacturing) rather than Marketing.

    • 15 Cost Analysis

    • Used alongside Value Chain to see where costs actually sit and whether they're justified by the value created there.
    • Cost drivers: scale, learning/experience curve, capacity utilization, linkages between activities, timing, location, policy choices.
    • Cost Leadership strategy = systematically drive down cost across the value chain while keeping acceptable quality → compete on price.
    • Ex: a momo cart cutting cost by sourcing veggies directly from a mandi (Procurement) instead of a middleman.

    16 Competitive Advantage & Outcomes

    • Competitive Advantage = delivering more value to customers than rivals, at a cost that's still profitable — the end-goal every framework above (VRIO, Value Chain, Five Forces) feeds into.
    • Two generic routes (Porter): Cost Leadership (lowest cost in industry) or Differentiation (unique value customers will pay more for). Trying to do both without a clear edge = "stuck in the middle."
    • Outcomes of sustained advantage: above-average returns/profitability, market share growth, brand equity, pricing power.
    • Advantage is temporary by default — must be renewed (via dynamic capabilities, continuous evaluation — links back to Principle 10).
    • Ex: Haldiram's — differentiation (trusted taste/hygiene) + cost efficiency (scale) together, not just one.

    Numericals practice

    Q1. Fixed costs ₹40,000/month, profit per unit ₹50. Find break-even units/month & per day.

    Break-even = 40,000 ÷ 50 = 800 units/month ≈ 27/day (÷30).

    Q2. Revenue ₹2,40,000, COGS is 40% of revenue, fixed cost ₹30,000. Find net profit.

    COGS = 0.40 × 2,40,000 = 96,000. Net Profit = 2,40,000 − 96,000 − 30,000 = ₹1,14,000.

    Q3. 100 plates/day @ ₹80, 5% MoM growth — find Month 2 plates/day and revenue (₹80/plate, 30 days).

    Plates: 100×1.05 = 105/day. Monthly revenue = 105×30×80 = ₹2,52,000.

    Q4. If a rival cart opens within 50m, growth forecast is cut by 2-3%. Original growth 8%, apply cut — new growth rate?

    New growth ≈ 8% − 2.5% (mid) = ~5.5% (accept 5–6% range).

    Short & Long Answer Practice

    Short Answer (2-3 lines)
    Differentiate Policy vs Procedure with an example.

    A Policy is a standing guideline/boundary with indefinite duration (e.g. "only fresh ingredients"). A Procedure is a fixed, repeatable step-by-step method (e.g. wash → steam → plate → serve). Policy sets the rule; Procedure is the exact routine followed every time to stay inside it.

    Why is Threat of Substitutes the "most overlooked" force?

    Because it isn't a direct competitor selling the same product — it's a different product solving the same customer need, so it's easy to miss while watching rivals in your own category (e.g. a momo cart losing customers to a sandwich stall, not another momo seller).

    Give one reason a corporate-level mistake is costlier than a functional one.

    A corporate-level mistake (e.g. entering the wrong business entirely) commits large capital and resources across the whole firm and is hard/slow to reverse; a functional mistake (e.g. a bad ad campaign) is contained to one department and far cheaper/faster to fix.

    What's the difference between a Goal and an Objective?

    A Goal is a long-term, semi-broad aim (e.g. "become the top regional dairy brand"). An Objective is specific and measurable, and changes more often (e.g. "increase market share by 5% this quarter"). Goals set direction; Objectives are the concrete, trackable targets under that direction.

    Long Answer (case-style, 8-10 lines)
    Take any Indian business you know. Identify its Mission, one Goal, one Objective, one Policy, one Program, one Procedure, a rough Budget, and one Tactic — show how they all trace back to the mission.

    Model answer (using Amul): Mission — "fair returns for farmers" (why it exists, unchanged since 1946). Goal — become India's largest and most trusted dairy cooperative. Objective — expand milk procurement volume by X% this year. Policy — only procure from registered farmer cooperatives at fixed fair prices. Program — a 6-month rural cold-chain expansion drive. Procedure — the fixed daily steps for milk collection, testing, and chilling at each village center. Budget — capital allocated for new chilling units this year. Tactic — a short festive-season ad push for Amul ice cream. Each layer narrows the one above it — Mission stays fixed at the top, Tactic is the fastest-changing, most ground-level move — and every layer must trace back to "fair returns for farmers," or it's a contradiction, not a strategy.

    Apply Porter's Five Forces to any industry of your choice (rate each Low/Mod/High/Very High with 1-line reasoning each), then conclude on overall attractiveness.

    Model answer (using Indian quick-commerce/instant delivery): New Entrants — High: low tech barrier, VC funding readily available. Buyer Power — High: customers switch apps instantly for better pricing/speed, near-zero switching cost. Supplier Power — Low/Mod: many FMCG/grocery suppliers compete for shelf space on these platforms. Rivalry — Very High: Zepto/Blinkit/Instamart in constant price and speed wars. Substitutes — High: local kirana stores, large-basket weekly grocery runs. Conclusion: an unattractive industry structurally (four of five forces High/Very High) — profitability depends on scale and operational efficiency, not the category itself.

    Explain the 4-stage Strategic Management Model using a real or hypothetical business decision, one stage at a time.

    Model answer (Priya's food delivery add-on): 1. Scan — she notices rainy-day walk-in drop-offs and hears hostel students complaining about food options (outward) while reviewing her own idle evening capacity (inward). 2. Formulate — she designs a plan: test delivery to 2 nearby hostels, decide pricing and delivery windows, weigh the trade-off of extra labor cost vs potential new revenue. 3. Implement — she runs the pilot for 2 weeks, actually taking orders and delivering. 4. Evaluate — she tracks order volume and margins after the pilot; results are good, so she expands to more hostels — which loops back into a fresh Scan of the next opportunity. The model is cyclic, not a one-time exercise — each Evaluate feeds the next Scan.

    Build a SWOT for a business you know, then explain 2 ways it differs from a Five Forces analysis of the same industry.

    Model answer (using a local momo cart): Strengths — low overhead, loyal regulars, fast service. Weaknesses — no fixed location, weather-dependent, no hygiene certification. Opportunities — a new hostel opening nearby, food-delivery app partnerships. Threats — a rival cart opening within 50m, rising LPG/ingredient costs. Difference 1 — Scope: SWOT looks at this one business specifically (internal + external mixed together); Five Forces looks at the whole street-food category's structure, ignoring this cart's individual strengths/weaknesses. Difference 2 — Use: SWOT is a quick, personal starting point any single owner can sketch in minutes; Five Forces is a more rigorous tool for comparing attractiveness across entire categories/industries, useful before even entering a business.

    UNIT 2 — Strategy Formulation & Strategic Alternatives · Sessions 18–23

    1 Introduction to Strategy Formulation

    • Once Unit 1 tells you WHERE you stand (SWOT, Five Forces), Unit 2 answers WHERE to go and HOW.
    • Strategy Formulation = deciding WHAT to do next — the planning/choice stage, as opposed to Implementation (actually DOING it: execution, resource allocation, structure).
    • Formulation failures are strategic; implementation failures are operational — but a brilliant formulation with poor implementation still fails.
    • Mainly driven by top/senior management (corporate & SBU level), fed by inputs from functional managers and environmental scanning.
    • Everything from Unit 1 — Mission, SWOT, Five Forces, PESTLE, VRIO/Core Competencies — is raw material for this stage.

    2 The Strategic Formulation Process

    StepWhat happens
    1. Environmental AnalysisRevisit external (PESTLE, Five Forces) + internal (SWOT, VRIO, Value Chain) — confirm where the firm stands.
    2. Revisit ObjectivesCheck Mission/Goals/Objectives are still realistic given the environment; revise if not.
    3. Generate AlternativesGrand Strategy Alternatives, TOWS, Ansoff, BCG/GE-McKinsey — generate real options.
    4. Evaluate AlternativesTest suitability (fits environment/capability?), feasibility (resources/time), acceptability (risk/return).
    5. Select the StrategyPick the best risk/return balance; large firms may pick a Combination across SBUs.
    6. Prepare for ImplementationTranslate choice back into Policies/Programs/Procedures/Budgets/Tactics (Unit 1 blocks).
  • ⚡ It's iterative, not linear — Step 4 (Evaluate) can send you back to Step 3, and the whole loop restarts whenever the environment shifts (same cyclic logic as the Strategic Mgmt Model).

    • 3 Grand Strategy Alternatives

    Four broad directions. Choice depends on: industry attractiveness, competitive position, resources, risk appetite.

    StrategyDefinitionChoose WhenExample
    StabilityKeep doing what works, same products/markets/scaleStable environment; growth would strain qualityFamily-run sweets brands staying regional deliberately
    GrowthExpand sales, share, assets, or marketsStrong position; resources availableReliance Jio's 2016 data bet
    RetrenchmentShrink, exit, or restructureLosing money, relevance, or competitivenessJet Airways cutting routes (too late)
    CombinationDifferent moves across different BUs simultaneouslyLarge, diversified conglomeratesTata: growing TCS & Tata Motors, divesting Teleservices
  • Growth is NOT the default — it's a deliberate choice.
  • Retrenchment chosen early = strategy. Forced late = collapse (Jet Airways 2019).
  • Unit 1 feeds this: Five Forces brutal → Stability/Retrenchment; strong SWOT strength → Growth.

    • 4 TOWS Matrix

  • Developed by Heinz Weihrich (1982). SWOT = diagnosis (what's true). TOWS = prescription (what to DO about it).
  • "TOWS" deliberately starts from outside (T, O) before looking inside (W, S).
    • SO — Maxi-Maxi ⚔ ATTACKUse Strengths to capture Opportunities. Amul: cooperative supply chain → expanded into ice cream/cheese (urban dairy demand)
    ST — Maxi-Mini 🛡 DEFENDUse Strengths to neutralise Threats. Indian IT: English-speaking talent → US/UK offices to blunt anti-outsourcing backlash
    WO — Mini-Maxi 🔧 FIX & GROWUse Opportunities to fix Weaknesses. Ola Electric: high fuel costs (W) + FAME II subsidies (O) → launched EV division
    WT — Mini-Mini 🆘 SURVIVEMinimise Weaknesses, avoid Threats. Jet Airways: should've sold assets/cut routes/found a partner. Didn't. Collapsed 2019.

    Limitations: only as good as the SWOT feeding it; subjective (same SWOT → different strategies); doesn't prioritize/rank; static — must be revisited.

    5 Ansoff Matrix

    Igor Ansoff, HBR 1957. If growth is the right call — HOW do we grow? Two questions: existing/new product × existing/new market. Risk rises the further you move from what you already know.

    EXISTING PRODUCTNEW PRODUCT
    EXISTING MARKETMarket Penetration — sell more of same to same customers. Ex: Colgate "brush twice a day". Risk: LowestProduct Development — new product to existing customers. Ex: Maggi adding oats noodles. Risk: Medium-High
    NEW MARKETMarket Development — same product to new customers/geo. Ex: Haldiram's going pan-India & abroad. Risk: MediumDiversification — new product + new market. Ex: ITC: cigarettes → hotels, FMCG. Risk: Highest

    Risk ladder: Market Penetration → Market Development → Product Development → Diversification (lowest → highest).

  • ⚡ Common mistake: companies chase Diversification to escape a broken core business — wrong. Fix the core first.

    • 6 Diversification — Related vs Unrelated

    RelatedNew business shares something REAL — customers, distribution, brand, raw material. Genuine synergy. Nike: shoes → apparel via same retail channels
    UnrelatedNew business shares NOTHING operational — only capital under the same owner. Riskier. Virgin: music → airlines → health clubs

    ITC (must know): 1910 tobacco only → 1970s–90s hotels & paperboards (Related, shared inputs/skills) → 2000s FMCG (Aashirvaad, Sunfeast, Bingo) using distribution reach + cigarette cash to fund years of losses.

  • Why diversification usually fails: no real synergy (just founder's interest); underestimating how different the new business is; starving new OR old business of resources; no patience for the payback period.

    • 7 BCG Matrix

    Boston Consulting Group, 1970. Axes: Market Growth Rate × Relative Market Share. For diversified firms — decide where to invest/hold/exit across SBUs. Cash Cows fund Stars. Divest Dogs. Nurture Question Marks selectively.

    HIGH MARKET SHARELOW MARKET SHARE
    HIGH GROWTH⭐ Stars — Invest heavily, future Cash Cows. Zenith Fleet❓ Question Marks — Invest selectively or exit. Zenith E-Volt

    8 GE-McKinsey Matrix

  • GE + McKinsey, early 1970s. Richer alternative to BCG — two composite, weighted scores instead of two raw numbers.
  • Y-axis: Industry Attractiveness (size, growth, intensity, margins, tech, regulation). X-axis: Business Strength (share, brand, cost position, distribution, capability, mgmt quality).
  • Output: 3×3 grid = 9 cells collapsing into 3 zones.
    • 🟢 INVEST / GROWHigh/High, High/Medium
    🟡 SELECTIVE / HOLDMedium/High, High/Low, Medium/Medium, Low/High
    🔴 HARVEST / DIVESTMedium/Low, Low/Medium, Low/Low

    Scoring steps (numericals!): list 5–6 factors per axis → assign weights per axis summing to 1.0 → score each factor 1(poor)–5(excellent) → weight × score per factor, sum → plot on the 3×3 grid.

    BCG = fast, objective, 2 hard numbers. GE = richer, judgment-based, weighted factors — handles nuance BCG misses, but more complex/time-consuming and scores can be gamed or biased.

    9 Product Life Cycle (PLC)

    Levitt (1965). Every product moves Introduction → Growth → Maturity → Decline; strategy must shift each stage. PLC explains WHY SBUs move between BCG quadrants over time — adds the time dimension BCG lacks.

    StageSalesStrategyBCG LinkExample
    IntroductionLow, loss-makingHigh awareness spend, prove categoryQuestion Mark ❓Zepto
    GrowthRising fastGrab share before saturationStar ⭐UPI apps 2018–21
    MaturityFlat/peakDefend share, extend product lifeCash Cow 🐄Parle-G biscuits
    DeclineFallingMilk remaining demand or exitDog 🐕Nokia feature phones
  • Maturity ≠ end — relaunch, new variants/use-cases can extend the cycle (ex: Maggi's many variants).
  • Limitations: not all products follow a neat curve; easier to spot the stage in hindsight than real-time; external shocks (new tech, pandemic) override the curve.

    Short & Long Answer Practice Unit 2

    Short Answer (2-3 lines)
    Differentiate Strategy Formulation from Strategy Implementation.

    Formulation = deciding WHAT to do — planning, choosing among alternatives (TOWS, Ansoff, BCG etc.), mainly top/senior management. Implementation = actually DOING it — executing the plan via resource allocation, structure, and day-to-day operations. A brilliant formulation with poor implementation still fails.

    Why does TOWS deliberately start from the outside (Threats/Opportunities) rather than the inside?

    Because external conditions (what the market/environment is doing) ultimately decide which internal strengths or weaknesses even matter — starting outside forces you to ask "what does the environment demand?" before asking "what can we do?", avoiding strategies that look good internally but ignore where the real opportunity or danger actually is.

    Give one reason Diversification is the riskiest Ansoff quadrant.

    It combines a new product with a new market simultaneously — the firm has no existing customer relationships, no proven product-market fit, and no fallback if either assumption is wrong, unlike the other three quadrants which keep at least one variable (product or market) familiar.

    What does PLC add to the BCG Matrix that BCG alone lacks?

    PLC adds the time dimension — BCG is a snapshot of share/growth at one moment, while PLC explains WHY and WHEN an SBU moves between BCG quadrants over its life (Question Mark → Star → Cash Cow → Dog), showing the trajectory rather than just the current position.

    Long Answer (case-style, 8-10 lines)
    Take any Indian business. Walk it through all 6 steps of the Strategic Formulation Process, one stage at a time.

    Model answer (using Haldiram's considering a new EV-delivery fleet): 1. Environmental Analysis — scans rising fuel costs and urban EV subsidies (PESTLE) alongside its own strong cash reserves (SWOT). 2. Revisit Objectives — confirms its logistics-cost objective is still realistic given fuel volatility. 3.     Compare BCG vs GE-McKinsey for a company with 5+ SBUs — when would you prefer the more complex GE model?

    BCG is fast and objective — just two hard numbers (share, growth) — good for a quick first-pass portfolio view. GE-McKinsey uses weighted, multi-factor composite scores on both axes (Industry Attractiveness, Business Strength), capturing nuance BCG's two raw numbers miss (e.g. regulation, brand, management quality). Prefer GE-McKinsey when: the company has 5+ SBUs with genuinely different competitive dynamics (not just differing in share/growth), when qualitative factors like regulation or technology shifts matter heavily, or when a board needs a more defensible, documented rationale for big capital-allocation calls — the added complexity and time cost is worth it precisely because more is riding on getting the call right.

    💯 Numericals — practice (break-even, GE-McKinsey scoring, SBU revenue)

    Q1. A firm sells a product for ₹500. Variable cost per unit is ₹300, and annual fixed costs are ₹10,00,000. Find the break-even quantity.

    Break-even = Fixed Costs ÷ (Price − Variable Cost) = 10,00,000 ÷ (500 − 300) = 10,00,000 ÷ 200 = 5,000 units.

    Q2. A company sells 10,000 units at ₹200 each, with variable costs of ₹120/unit and fixed costs of ₹4,00,000. Find the net profit.

    Contribution/unit = 200 − 120 = ₹80. Total contribution = 10,000 × 80 = 8,00,000. Net Profit = 8,00,000 − 4,00,000 = ₹4,00,000.

    Q3. A firm's revenue is ₹2,40,000, COGS is 40% of revenue, and fixed costs are ₹30,000. Find the net profit.

    COGS = 0.40 × 2,40,000 = 96,000. Net Profit = 2,40,000 − 96,000 − 30,000 = ₹1,14,000.

    Q4. Zenith Motors' annual enterprise revenue is $14.2B, down 6% YoY. Zenith Fleet holds a 15% revenue share. What was Fleet's revenue, and what was total revenue a year ago (before the 6% drop)?

    Fleet revenue = 15% × 14.2B = $2.13B. Revenue a year ago = 14.2B ÷ (1 − 0.06) = 14.2B ÷ 0.94 ≈ $15.11B.

    Q5 (GE-McKinsey weighted scoring). An SBU is scored on Industry Attractiveness using 3 factors: Market Size (weight 0.5, score 4), Growth Rate (weight 0.3, score 5), Regulation (weight 0.2, score 2). Find the final Industry Attractiveness score, and classify it High/Medium/Low (High ≥3.5, Medium 2.5–3.5, Low <2.5).

    Score = (0.5×4) + (0.3×5) + (0.2×2) = 2.0 + 1.5 + 0.4 = 3.9 → High attractiveness (matches the method used for Zenith E-Volt's 4.6 score).

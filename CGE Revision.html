Corporate Governance & Ethics — Midsem Revision

Modules 1–5, up to Governance of Family Businesses · Exam: Monday · Short + Long + Case-based
MODULE 1 — Foundations & Key Principles · Weeks 1–2

1 What is Corporate Governance?

  • Corporate Governance (CG) = the system of rules and processes by which a company is directed and controlled. In plain words: the rulebook for who decides what, and who watches the decision-makers.
  • Governance vs Management — the single most-tested distinction: Management runs the day-to-day business (operations). Governance makes sure the business is run correctly, ethically, and in the right direction (oversight). Management drives the car; governance checks the driver isn't drunk and is going the agreed route.
  • Primary objective: balance the competing interests of everyone with a stake in the company — not just shareholders.

2 TAFR — the four pillars

The core framework of the whole course. Any governance failure can usually be traced to one of these breaking.

TransparencyClear, accurate, timely disclosure of financial and operational info to everyone who needs it.
AccountabilityBoard and management are held directly responsible for their decisions and outcomes — someone must answer for it.
FairnessEqual treatment of all stakeholders, especially minority shareholders (the small guys who can't fight back).
ResponsibilityFollowing legal, environmental and social obligations — the price of being allowed to operate.

3 Why CG matters

  • Capital protection & investor confidence — good governance lowers the cost of raising money (investors trust you more, so ask for less in return).
  • Mitigates systemic risk — catches fraud, founder over-dominance, misconduct before they snowball into a crisis.
  • Long-term value — shifts focus from "win this quarter" to sustainable, decades-long growth.
  • Institutional trust — protects brand reputation with customers, suppliers, regulators.

4 The 8 stakeholders

For any case-based question ("who is affected by this failure?"), run through this list — it's the single most reusable tool in the course.

MODULE 2 — Theoretical Foundations · Weeks 2–3

1 Agency Theory vs Stewardship Theory

These two theories answer: "should we trust the people running the company, or watch them closely?" — and this exact pair comes back in the family-business module, so learn it well once.

Agency TheoryStewardship Theory
Core ideaOwners (principals) and managers (agents) are separate people with separate interests — managers may drift toward their own benefit.Managers aren't selfish agents — they're stewards who genuinely identify with the company's success.
AssumesSelf-interest, possible opportunismCollective-minded, trustworthy
FixMonitor: board oversight, audits, pay tied to performanceEmpower: give autonomy, board acts as adviser not watchdog
Cost of getting it wrong— Over-monitoring a genuine steward kills their motivation; trusting an agent too much opens the door to abuse

Rule of thumb carried into Module 5: "design for stewards, install tripwires for agents."

2 Stakeholder Theory & Resource Dependency Theory

Stakeholder TheoryA company owes ethical duties to everyone affected by it — not just shareholders. Long-term survival = balancing all 8 stakeholder groups, not maximising short-term shareholder value alone. Governance tools: ESG reporting, community engagement, ethical supply chains.
Resource Dependency TheoryCompanies depend on the outside world for resources (capital, legal access, networks). The Board's job here isn't just oversight — it's a bridge to those resources. Directors bring: expertise/advice, reputation & network, and regulatory access.

3 Transaction Cost Theory & Political/Managerial Hegemony Theory

Transaction Cost TheoryMarkets have friction — bargaining, monitoring and enforcing deals all cost something. Firms build internal controls (contracts, audit committees) because that's cheaper than constantly re-negotiating with outsiders. Explains why formal governance structures exist at all.
Managerial Hegemony TheoryEven though the Board is legally in charge, in practice management (the CEO/founder) often controls the agenda and board appointments — the board becomes a passive "rubber stamp." This is exactly why independent-director rules exist — to counter natural managerial dominance.

Political Theory of Governance — views governance as a distribution of power among shareholders, managers, employees and regulators; highlights how legal frameworks and voting rights shape who really decides.

4 Law & courts, in brief

No single file covers "law and court interventions" as a standalone topic, but these legal reference points recur across every case in this course — know them as quick facts:

  • Companies Act, Section 197 (India) — caps a public company's managerial pay at ~11% of net profits without shareholder approval. Eicher Motors 2021: shareholders voted down the promoter-MD's pay hike.
  • SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India) — requires Audit Committee approval for related-party transactions, with extra approval for large ("material") ones.
  • RBI rule (2022) — would have forced Tata Sons to list publicly; Tata Sons restructured instead to avoid it.
  • Courts/regulators step in when a company's internal governance fails: contested EGMs (Byju's 2024, investors voted to remove founders), insolvency proceedings, forensic reviews (Fortis 2018), and fraud convictions (Adelphia, Satyam).
MODULE 3 — Board of Directors & Internal Controls · Weeks 3–5

1 What the Board actually does

  • The Board is the bridge between shareholders (owners) and management (operators) — Owners elect → Board oversees/approves → Management executes.
  • Primary fiduciary duty: strategic oversight, evaluating executives, managing risk — on behalf of the company, not any one person who appointed them.
  • Six things a good board should actually do: Strategy (approve direction), Leadership (hire/evaluate CEO), Capital (approve fundraising/major spend), Risk & controls, Conflicts (handle related-party deals), Accountability (track disclosures/culture).
  • Rule of thumb: if a decision can materially change ownership, survival, leadership, reputation or risk → the board should see it.

2 Types of directors

TypeWhat it meansAddsWatch-out
ExecutiveAlso works in management (e.g. founder-CEO)Deep business contextCan dominate discussion
Non-executiveNo day-to-day executive roleDistance, broader viewMay lack operating detail
IndependentMeets legal independence criteria (no material/financial ties)Objective challenge, trustIndependence must be real, not cosmetic
NomineeNominated by an investor/institutionInvestor context, capital disciplineDuty is still to the company, not the investor

A board ≠ management ≠ advisors. Only the Board has formal legal voting rights and fiduciary duty; advisors just advise, with no vote.

3 Board committees

Boards delegate specialised oversight to committees so nothing falls through the cracks.

Audit CommitteeOversees financial reporting integrity, internal controls, and the external auditor.
Nomination & Remuneration (NRC)Sets executive pay, manages CEO succession, evaluates directors.
Risk & ComplianceIdentifies operational, financial, legal and environmental risks.

Value: prevents concentration of power, protects minority shareholders, enforces regulatory standards.

4 Internal controls, audit & whistleblowers

  • Internal Audit — an independent check of the company's own processes and risk management, done from inside.
  • External Audit — independent verification of financial statements, done by an outside firm, for investors/public assurance.
  • Whistleblower policy — a confidential channel for employees to report fraud/harassment without fear of retaliation. Acts as an early-warning system.

MODULE 4 — Disclosures & Corporate Disruptions · Weeks 6–7

1 Why disclosure matters

Disclosure exists to eliminate information asymmetry — the gap between what insiders (founders/management) know and what outsiders (investors, public) know. The bigger that gap, the more a failure can hide until it's too late (see: BYJU'S below).

2 Three categories of disclosure

Financial ReportingMandatory audited quarterly/annual results, debt covenants, cash flow statements.
Governance DisclosuresExecutive pay, board independence, related-party transactions.
Material Event ReportingImmediate disclosure of big developments — litigation, regulatory issues, leadership changes.

Enforced by market regulators (SEBI in India, SEC in the US) to protect capital markets and trust.

MODULE 5 — Governance of Family Businesses · Weeks 8–9 — your midsem cutoff

1 The Three-Ring Model (Tagiuri & Davis, HBS)

  • Before this model (late 1970s), "the family business" was treated as one tangled system, so every conflict looked personal. Tagiuri & Davis split it into three overlapping circles: Family Ownership Business.
  • A person can sit in 1, 2, or all 3 rings at once — giving 7 possible seats.
RingYou enter byFairness meansExit
FamilyBirth / marriageEveryone treated equallyPractically impossible
OwnershipBuying/inheriting sharesProportional to your stakeSell — if there's a buyer
BusinessHired for competenceReward by meritResign, or let go

The 7 seats (where you sit decides what you see — and what you ask for):

  • Family only → "Is this fair to all of us?"
  • Owner only → "What's my return, and when?"
  • Employee only → "Is there a ceiling for me here?"
  • Family+Owner → "Why no dividend this year?"
  • Owner+Employee → "Do I have real authority?"
  • Family+Employee → "Am I paid as family or staff?"
  • All three (founder) → "Who am I in this meeting?"

Big idea: most "personality conflicts" in a family firm are actually role conflicts — figure out which ring someone is speaking from before judging them. Rings start overlapping at founding, then drift apart as funding brings in owners and growth brings in non-family employees.

India examples: Nykaa (founder-mother + 2 children, IPO widened ownership), Zerodha (2 brothers, bootstrapped — all 3 rings nearly coincide), Honasa/Mamaearth (husband-wife co-founders), Murugappa Group (2020: a family shareholder sought a board seat and was refused — "seat 4 asking for a voice").

2 Why normal governance breaks in family firms

Classic corporate governance (Berle & Means, 1932) was designed for strangers — thousands of dispersed shareholders who never meet the managers they're trusting. In a family firm, the monitor and the monitored eat dinner together — so the whole "watch the manager" logic needs rethinking.

Kitchen-table logicBoardroom logic
Who belongsBy birth or marriageBy contribution/contract
Fairness meansEqual shares, need-basedMerit, proportional to stake
Decisions bySeniority, consensus, emotionData, vote, documented
Conflict isAvoided, or never forgottenSurfaced, resolved, minuted
Time horizonGenerationsQuarters, funding rounds
ExitUnthinkableExpected and planned

In India specifically: promoter-group culture (family control is formalised by law, not hidden), seniority/deference (hard to contradict an elder), joint-family/HUF asset holding (blurs individual ownership), and the "family WhatsApp group" — a shadow board with no minutes, closed to non-family executives.

BharatPe (2022): a co-founder's spouse served as the fintech's head of controls — the very role meant to check wrongdoing. An internal review alleged fund misuse; both she and the co-founder exited. Design rule: no family member in a role whose job is to check another family member.

3 Agency & Stewardship theory, applied to family firms

Module 2's two theories, re-applied — this link is a favourite long-answer question.

  • Agency problems shrink in one way: when the owner is the manager, the classic owner-vs-manager gap narrows.
  • But a new agency problem appears — "altruism costs": parents struggle to monitor/discipline children they love → free-riding, entitlement, nobody gets fired (Schulze et al., 2001).
  • And a bigger one grows: principal-vs-principal conflict — the controlling family vs minority owners (VCs, outside heirs, public shareholders). A VC-backed Indian family startup lives exactly here.
  • Stewardship / Socioemotional Wealth (SEW): families may value control, identity and legacy so much they accept lower returns or higher risk just to keep them (Gómez-Mejía et al., 2007).

Rule to remember: "design for stewards; install tripwires for agents" — early, while it's cheap.

4 Divergent objectives — one rupee, three claims

Every rupee of profit has three legitimate, competing claims on it — and it can only be spent once.

Businesswants to reinvest it — growth, competitive position.
Familywants to preserve it — control, reputation, legacy (socioemotional wealth).
Ownerswant to pay it out — dividends, buybacks, a fair exit/liquidity.

SEW = FIBER (what families protect): Family control · Identification (name on the door) · Binding ties (community/network) · Emotional attachment (company as a "child") · Renewal (hand it to next gen).

Tata Sons: Tata Trusts (~66%, wants control + dividends for philanthropy) vs SP Group/Mistry family (~18%, wants liquidity). A 2022 RBI rule would've forced a listing — Tata Sons restructured instead to avoid it.

Three clocks, one company: Business runs on quarters; a VC fund has a ~10-year life and needs an exit; a family thinks in generations (25/50/100 years). Taking VC money means agreeing to someone else's clock.

Dividend examples: Vedanta (big payouts despite the parent's heavy debt), Waltons/Walmart (stable, disclosed policy), Indian startups (no dividends — liquidity via ESOP buybacks/secondaries). Preservation examples: Samsung (inheritance tax, contested 2015 merger), Mars (private for a century), Hermès (family pooled shares to resist an LVMH takeover).

The big idea: conflicting goals aren't unethical. Hiding the trade-off is. Ethics fails when one party wins through concealment, capture, or silence.

5 Nepotism & tunnelling — when control becomes private benefit

Three tests for any family decision (use these directly in case-based answers):

  1. Surname swap — replace the relative with a stranger. Would the board approve the same deal on the same terms? (This is what the law calls "arm's length.")
  2. Consent — did the people who bear the cost know, and have a real chance to object?
  3. Headline test — would you be comfortable reading it on the front page, told by a skeptic?

Nepotism is a spectrum, not a switch: Pure entitlement → Preference with a floor → Beat the outside bar → No-family rule. Ethical question: is the policy explicit, agreed in advance, and applied to everyone?

Evidence it's costly: heir-CEOs linked to underperformance, mainly those without credentials (Pérez-González 2006, US); Danish family successions cut profitability ~4pp (Bennedsen et al. 2007); adopted heirs chosen on merit outperform blood heirs in Japan, e.g. Suzuki (Mehrotra et al. 2013). The problem is selection by birth, not family itself.

Nepotism's hidden tax — 4 groups who pay: minority investors (value loss), non-family talent (hit a ceiling, leave), the heir themself (never learns if they earned it), and other family members (grievance from being passed over).

Tunnelling / self-dealing (Johnson, La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes & Shleifer, 2000): value is moved out of the company into a family-owned entity — e.g. the company buys an asset from the family too expensively, or sells one too cheaply; everyday channels are rent, vendor contracts, payroll, royalties, loans, and cheap share issues to family. Always ask: "would a stranger get this exact deal?"

CaseWhat happened
Satyam (2008, India)Board approved buying two family-linked firms; investors revolted in a day; weeks later a massive accounting fraud was confessed.
United Spirits (2015, India)After Diageo took control, an inquiry found funds diverted to promoter-linked entities.
Fortis (2018, India)Loans to promoter-linked entities triggered the promoters' ouster and a forensic review.
Adelphia (2002, US)The Rigas family used the cable company to back billions in loans to family entities — bankruptcy + fraud convictions.
Parmalat (2003, Italy)The Tanzi family's dairy empire collapsed with a multi-billion-euro hole funnelled to family businesses.

Succession promises: Infosys — founders pledged their children wouldn't work there; a 2013 exception drew sharp criticism, both father & son left by 2014. Murdoch family — a 2024 attempt to change the family trust to favour one heir was blocked after three siblings objected.

6 Five guardrails against nepotism & tunnelling

1. Disinterested approvalOnly people with no stake in the deal can approve it.
2. Arm's-length benchmarksRent, contracts, pay checked against market rates.
3. DisclosureEvery related-party deal reported to the board, every year.
4. Family employment policyWritten down before any particular relative needs a job.
5. Independent committeesAudit/pay committees chaired by non-family members.

Pay rule: Companies Act s.197 caps managerial pay (~11% of net profit) without shareholder approval — Eicher Motors (2021): shareholders voted down a promoter-MD pay resolution.

⭐ Case: Shree Annapurna Foods

A private limited namkeen/snacks company (Indore, ~₹400 crore revenue). Founded 1972 by Ramesh Agrawal; became private limited in 1995 with his 3 sons joining. Ramesh died 2008 — shares split equally, 1/3rd per branch.

BranchHeadIn the businessFeels
ASuresh (eldest, Chairman)Suresh + son Vikram (CEO)Runs the company, assumes that's natural — others feel it's "Branch A's company"
BMaheshOnly Mahesh as director (wife Sunita, director on paper)Biggest branch, mostly outside the business, no dividend for 4 years — feels it's funding everyone else
CDineshDinesh + son Karan as directors; daughter Priya runs a D2C spinoff (Annapurna Direct)The entrepreneurial branch — Priya's startup makes others both proud and jealous

Governance today: 6 directors (2 per branch), no outsiders; meetings ~40 min, follow a family lunch where real decisions actually happen. 26 adult family members, 9 own shares but don't work in the business (mostly Branch B) — no family assembly, council or written constitution. Because it's a private limited company, the law requires no independent directors — but a retired FMCG CFO has been invited to "advise informally" and may become the first independent director.

How to analyse this with your frameworks: Map each person's ring/seat (Three-Ring Model) → spot the SEW/FIBER tension (Vikram wants to sell the emotionally-loaded old factory; Dinesh resists) → check Branch B's complaint using the surname-swap/consent tests (is "no dividend for 4 years while others get investment" fair, and did B consent?) → recommend the 5 guardrails (a written family employment/dividend policy, an independent director, disclosed related-party dealings — note Mahesh's own firm supplies all the company's packaging since 2001, a classic tunnelling-risk channel to flag).

⭐ Case: Saffron Grid — the COO decision

Fictional Pune cold-chain logistics startup, Series B, raising Series C in 9 months. Founder Rakesh wants to make his son Kabir (27, MBA abroad, 18 months at the company, never managed more than 5 people) COO — with Ananya (39, 6 years, took on-time delivery from 82% to 96%, investor-favourite) reporting to him.

Board: 5 seats — Rakesh, his wife Meena (non-exec), 2 VC nominees, 1 independent (chosen by Rakesh). Rule: CXO appointments pass by simple majority; related-party deals/relatives' pay need a VC veto. Proposed pay: Kabir ₹1.2cr vs Ananya's current ₹85L.

Model answer approach: Apply the surname-swap test — would anyone propose an unrelated 27-year-old with 18 months' experience as COO over a 6-year veteran who delivered results? No → this is nepotism, not a close call. Who pays: investors (valuation + trust ahead of Series C — this is also a related-party-transaction disclosure issue), employees (morale — the top rung looks family-only). The fix that should have existed already: a written family employment policy agreed years before anyone was personally affected. As the independent director, your duty is to the company, not to Rakesh.

⭐ Case: Byju's — a board that shrank to fit a family

YearEvent

Two readings, both defensible (great long-answer material): Agency reading — concentrated control + late accounts + an all-family board = nobody left to monitor; a textbook principal-vs-principal failure (minority investors couldn't protect themselves). 🔍 Case-method: how to research a related-party transaction

This is the method from your research assignment (boAt / Lenskart / HealthKart / Mamaearth) — it's essentially a worked template for any case-based exam question about founder/family conflicts of interest. Memorise the questions, not the companies.

Step 1 — Who built it? Founders, founding year & original idea, how the business model changed, key investors, when professional managers/independent directors arrived.

Step 2 — Follow the money. How much funding raised & from whom; what happened to founder ownership as outsiders came in; are founders still directors; does a founder-linked entity still do business with the company?

Step 3 — Look for the relationship. Search terms: Related Party, Promoter, Director, Key Management Personnel, Remuneration, Royalty, Rent, Loans/advances.

Step 4 — For one relationship, record: Who ↔ Who · what's the relationship · what's the transaction · why might the company need it (commercially useful) · why might an investor want it disclosed (conflict-of-interest risk) · who should approve/check it.

The exam-ready instinct this builds: never assume a related-party transaction is automatically wrong — ask what information an outside investor would want before trusting it, and who should be approving it (disinterested parties only, per the 5 guardrails).

Short Answer Practice from Quiz 1 — know these cold

Q. GoMechanic grew rapidly, but concerns emerged about the accuracy of financial reporting to investors. Which stakeholder was most directly affected? Give one reason.

Shareholders/Investors. Inaccurate financial reporting directly breaks the Transparency pillar of TAFR — investors can't make informed decisions (about valuation, further funding, or exit) if the numbers they're given aren't accurate.

Q. BYJU'S faced delayed reporting, debt problems and auditor/board resignations. Identify TWO stakeholders affected and one impact on each.

Creditors/Lenders: debt covenant breaches raise default risk and threaten repayment. Employees: financial distress threatens job security and unpaid dues/salaries (reports noted founders pledging personal assets to meet payroll).

Q. A founder says "I founded the company, so I should take all the important decisions myself." What governance risk does this create? Name one mechanism to reduce it.

Risk: founder over-dominance — concentrated decision-making with no check, a classic Agency/Managerial-Hegemony problem (the board becomes a rubber stamp). Mechanism: appoint independent directors with real (not cosmetic) independence, so decisions get genuine outside challenge.

Q. TAFR — Transparency means clear, timely information. Give one simple example of a startup demonstrating transparency to investors.

Sending investors accurate monthly/quarterly financial updates (cash burn, runway, key metrics) on a fixed schedule, rather than only when asked or when things look good.

Q. A startup gives one group of shareholders information about a major decision but not others. Which TAFR principle is compromised, and why?

Fairness. Fairness means equal treatment and protection of rights for all stakeholders, particularly minority shareholders — selectively sharing material information creates an unfair information advantage for the favoured group.

Q. A startup expands without following environmental regulations and local legal requirements. Which TAFR principle applies? Give one action to take.

Responsibility — adherence to legal, regulatory and environmental obligations is the "license to operate." Action: set up a statutory/legal compliance function (or compliance committee) before expanding into new jurisdictions.

Long Answer Practice 8–10 lines, framework + example

Explain Agency Theory and Stewardship Theory, and how family businesses need both lenses at once.

Model answer: Agency Theory (Jensen & Meckling, 1976) assumes managers (agents) may drift from owners' (principals') interests — pursuing personal perks or power over long-term value — so governance should monitor: independent boards, audits, performance-linked pay. Stewardship Theory (Davis, Schoorman & Donaldson, 1997) assumes the opposite: managers who identify with the firm's success need empowerment, not surveillance — over-monitoring a genuine steward can backfire by eroding trust and motivation. In a family firm, both apply simultaneously but to different relationships: when the owner is also the manager, the classic owner-manager gap shrinks (stewardship fits), but a new "altruism cost" problem appears — parents can't discipline their own children (Schulze et al., 2001) — and principal-vs-principal conflict grows between the controlling family and minority/VC owners (agency fits here). The practical rule that reconciles both: design governance to empower genuine stewards, but install tripwires — independent directors, disclosure rules, automatic triggers — for situations where agency risk is real, such as related-party deals or succession decisions.

Using the Three-Ring Model, explain why most family-business conflicts are "role conflicts dressed up as personality conflicts." Use an example.

Model answer: The Three-Ring Model (Tagiuri & Davis) splits a family firm into three overlapping systems — Family, Ownership, Business — each with its own entry rule, notion of fairness, and exit option. A person can occupy 1, 2 or all 3 rings, creating 7 possible seats, and each seat sees the same decision differently: a Family-only member (e.g. a non-working spouse) asks "is this fair to all of us?", while an Owner-only member (e.g. a VC) asks "what's my return, and when?" When these different logics collide — say, over a proposal to reinvest all profits instead of paying dividends — it looks like a personal clash, but it's really a structural one: each party is correctly defending the logic of their own ring. Example: in Shree Annapurna Foods, Branch B (Mahesh) has had no dividend for four years and feels like it's "funding everyone else's plans" — this isn't really Branch B being difficult, it's the Ownership ring's fairness logic (returns proportional to stake) clashing with the Business ring's logic (reinvest for growth). The fix isn't to declare one side right, but to build structures — a family council, a written dividend policy — that let each ring's legitimate interest be heard before conflict boils over.

What is "tunnelling"? Explain using one Indian and one global example, and list the guardrails that prevent it.

Model answer: Tunnelling (Johnson, La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes & Shleifer, 2000) is when a controlling family moves value out of a company they partly own into an entity they wholly own — through overpriced asset purchases, underpriced asset sales, inflated rent/royalties/fees, or cheap share issues to family. Because the family owns 100% of the second entity but only part of the first, every rupee tunnelled out benefits them at minority shareholders' direct expense. Indian example: Fortis (2018) — loans to promoter-linked entities triggered the promoters' ouster and a forensic review. Global example: Adelphia (2002, US) — the Rigas family used the cable company to back billions in loans to their own family entities, leading to bankruptcy and fraud convictions. Guardrails: (1) disinterested approval — only people with no stake in the deal can approve it; (2) arm's-length benchmarking against market rates; (3) mandatory disclosure of every related-party deal to the board annually; (4) a written family-employment/dealing policy set before anyone is personally affected; (5) independent audit and remuneration committees chaired by non-family members. The simplest practical test is the "surname swap": would the board approve this exact deal for a complete stranger?

Case-Based Practice apply the frameworks to a fresh scenario

A private family company has 3 branches, unequal involvement in the business, and no written family constitution. One branch's founder-linked firm supplies the company's raw materials at above-market rates, approved informally by the branch head who chairs the board. How would you analyse this?

Model answer structure: (1) Map the people onto the Three-Ring Model — who's Family-only, Family+Owner, Family+Owner+Employee — to see whose voice is structurally missing (usually the branches outside daily operations). (2) Apply the surname-swap test to the supply contract: would the board approve above-market rates from an unrelated vendor? No → this is tunnelling risk, not a normal commercial deal. (3) Note the conflict of interest: the same person who benefits from the deal (branch head) is approving it — this breaks disinterested approval, guardrail #1. (4) Recommend fixes from the 5 guardrails: independent director/committee approval for all related-party deals, market-rate benchmarking, mandatory annual disclosure, and a written related-party-transaction policy. (5) Flag which stakeholders are harmed — minority family owners (reduced profit share) and, if outside investors exist, their capital protection interest too.

A startup founder wants to appoint his daughter as Head of Internal Controls because he trusts her completely. Evaluate this from a governance perspective.

Model answer structure: Directly invokes the BharatPe design rule from this course: no family member should hold a role whose job is to check another family member — because it collapses the separation between "the monitor and the monitored" that governance depends on. Even under Stewardship Theory (assume she's genuinely well-intentioned), the structural problem remains: an internal-controls head who can't realistically investigate her own father/relatives has no real independence, regardless of personal integrity — this is an Agency Theory blind spot the trust-based framing ignores. Recommend: keep the daughter in a role without oversight authority over family members, or appoint an independent/non-family person to Internal Controls, with the family employment policy (guardrail #4) written down so this is decided by principle, not personal exception.
